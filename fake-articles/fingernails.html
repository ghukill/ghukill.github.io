Abstract

Background: Office workers worldwide have long observed the inexplicable tendency of freshly clipped fingernail fragments to migrate toward computer keyboards with remarkable consistency. Despite decades of anecdotal evidence, no rigorous scientific investigation has been conducted to understand this phenomenon.

Methods: We employed high-speed cameras, electromagnetic field detectors, and quantum gravitometers to analyze the trajectories of 2,847 fingernail clippings across 156 office environments. Keyboards were categorized by material composition, age, and accumulated debris mass. Statistical analysis was performed using the Keratin-Keyboard Correlation Coefficient (KKCC).

Results: Our findings demonstrate a statistically significant attraction force (p < 0.001) between keratin fragments and silicon-based input devices. The attraction strength correlates positively with keyboard surface area (r = 0.89) and inversely with office cleanliness index (r = -0.72). Mechanical keyboards showed 340% greater attractive force compared to membrane alternatives. The phenomenon appears to operate through a previously undiscovered subset of gravitational force, which we term "Digital Gravitational Bias" (DGB).

Conclusions: This study provides the first empirical evidence for the existence of DGB, suggesting that the same fundamental forces governing planetary motion also influence the migration patterns of personal grooming debris in office environments. These findings have profound implications for workplace hygiene protocols and may revolutionize our understanding of quantum mechanics at the macro scale.
